A photographer (1882–1960) was an official photographer for a national reclamation service from the early 1900s to late 1910s. During these years, this individual took thousands of photographs documenting the reclamation service's irrigation projects across the western United States. This person recorded the progress of construction projects as well as machinery and personnel. The agency also asked this individual to photograph nearby towns and farms for a series of articles designed to promote settlement on land reclaimed from the desert through irrigation. Traveling with a large camera and glass-plate negatives, this person documented numerous projects in several western states. After leaving the reclamation service in the late 1910s, this individual left professional photography until the 1930s, when this person photographed the building of a major dam. The photographs capture both engineering feats and everyday life in the early 20th-century West. The optimistic images impress the viewer with the technological and social advances made by people in the West. They make the point that progress and community had come to isolated, formerly barren places, and that abundant opportunities awaited those willing to move west and work hard on the reclaimed land. This individual, born the fourth of six children in 1882 in a city in Idaho, was from a family led by a German immigrant from New York City. This person married Alice, a divorcee, whose parents were prominent figures, in early December 1911 in Idaho. They had no children, but this photographer did assist in the raising of Alice's only child by her former marriage. After photography, this individual went back to a former career working as a dry goods store clerk, a profession apparently held in higher regard than the short stint with fame as the "Government Photographer," and died quietly in this person's home town of Boise some time in mid-1960 at an old age according to a July 1960 report.
