James Carter, born on 4 June 1913, was a prominent engineer who contributed significantly to both academia and industry. He studied at the University of Leeds and decided to pursue a bachelor's degree in civil engineering. Carter obtained a master's degree in advanced structural dynamics from Manchester in 1930, and in the same year, he took up the chair in Structural Engineering at the University of Sheffield. He transitioned to working for a consulting engineering firm based in the Midlands and later joined a regional water management strategy team.

During the early stages of a global conflict in the 20th century, Carter studied engineering tactics and was involved with a government air division. After a major conflict, he joined an aerospace engineering consultancy. He participated in technology safety cases and contributed to the evaluation of airship design methodologies, but transitioned away from aviation engineering following a high-profile aviation incident. Carter continued his teaching career at Imperial College during the Second World War.

Carter was active in various engineering bodies including a national engineering association and served as vice-chancellor of a leading technical institute the year after he became a Fellow of the Royal Academy of Engineering in 1955. He presided over a worldwide engineering consortium for a session in the late 1950s and chaired research into national waterway pollution. Despite facing media criticism for the slow pace of national waterway clean-up efforts, he maintained a significant academic presence at various polytechnics.

Towards the end of his career, James Carter was a senior consultant on the Advisory Research Board during the late 20th century and, upon his retirement in 1965, began a lecture series in North America. He received honorary degrees from leading European technical universities before passing away on 8 February 1989.
