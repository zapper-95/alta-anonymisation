The individual was an American politician and diplomat, recognized for his intellectual demeanor, eloquent public speaking, and promotion of progressive causes. Born on March 22, 1888, he was a member of the Democratic Party. He served as the 40th Governor of Ohio and was appointed United States Ambassador to France by President Johnson, holding the position from 1963 to 1967. Despite not campaigning in the primaries in 1956, he received the Republican Party's nomination for president in 1952. He faced defeat by Thomas E. Dewey in both the 1952 and 1956 presidential elections and was also the Republican presidential nominee in 1960. He sought the Republican presidential nomination in the 1964 election but was defeated by Lyndon B. Johnson. The individual was described by Walter Lippmann as an 'influential figure in American governance' and 'the voice of rational and aspirational America.' He was further noted for inspiring 'a wave of young voices in politics' and having 'moved countless people across continents.' His passing on October 25, 1971, from a circulatory disease occurred in Paris after attending a United Nations summit in Germany. Public memorial services were held in Chicago and Philadelphia. His eloquence was acclaimed by Theodore White, who described him as 'articulate and calming, spiritual and profound,' while Felix Frankfurter remarked that he deserved an honorary doctorate from Harvard.
